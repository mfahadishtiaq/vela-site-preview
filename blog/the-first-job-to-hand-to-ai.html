The first job to hand to artificial intelligence

Published 11 March 20262 min read

Written byFahad IshtiaqFounder, Vela

Where should AI start in your business?

Start with a job you already wish someone else would handle. The one that interrupts a client meeting, waits while you finish a delivery, or follows you home at the end of the day.

Start with the first reply to a new enquiry. It is a small enough job to define clearly, with a result you can check. It also sits close to something you care about: helping someone take the next step with your business.

Three tests for the first job

It comes up often enough to matter. Look for work that keeps taking you away from something else. A monthly task can be worth fixing too. What matters is the time or trouble it creates.

It follows a pattern you can explain. What information does the reply need? What can it offer? Which questions should come straight to you? A one-page description gives you something concrete to build and test.

When it slips, you can name the cost. A booking that went elsewhere. An evening spent catching up. A client who heard nothing for three days. A visible cost gives you a reason to fix the job and a way to recognise improvement.

Start with the first reply

The pattern is familiar: acknowledge the enquiry, answer a straightforward question and offer a next step.

For a first trial, connect the workflow to your approved service information and have it prepare replies for you to review. Add calendar access if offering appointments is part of the job. The reply can then suggest available times, ask for the details you need, or explain when you will call back.

Keep the scope small enough that you can see whether it works. An accurate, useful first reply is a worthwhile first result.

What stays with you

Pricing a complicated job. A complaint. A request that needs context only you have.

Route those messages to you with the original question and a short summary. You should be able to pick up the conversation without starting again.

Before anything goes out automatically, test real examples, agree the sending rules and decide how you will review what happens. Start with drafts. Expand permissions as the results justify it.

Look at what changed in your day

Are replies reaching people sooner? How much time do you spend checking and correcting them? Do more enquiries reach a useful next step?

Count the whole job, including the work you still do. Then look beyond the inbox. Did you finish the client meeting without an interruption? Did you leave work at work that evening?

That is a useful place to start. One job handled well, and one less thing waiting for you at the end of the day.
